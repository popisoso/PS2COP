The Challenge

Some members of the Community of Practice, ‘Kracht(vrouwen) in de wijk’, work not only with adult women but also with young girls. Often, these are mothers and their daughters from the same neighborhood. Through this direct involvement, participants gain valuable insights into the living environments of both generations. At the same time, several members notice that a gap is emerging between these two worlds. Differences in values, expectations, and communication cause tension or misunderstandings. As a professional working with both groups, you find yourself right in the middle of these generations.

How do you unite different generations?

From Experience: What (Not) To Do

  • Participants of ‘Kracht(vrouwen) in de wijk’ see in their work that young people often have questions their parents are unaware of. Whether or not the parents are informed, they often don’t know how to address these topics.
  • A gap between parents and growing adolescents has always existed. However, issues such as social media, influencers, the COVID-19 pandemic, and growing up with discrimination make this era different. The challenges parents and young people face have changed accordingly.
  • The fact that young people (and often mothers) turn to these participants and confide in them is a sign that these organizations provide safe spaces for them.
  • These participants are seen as trusted figures, which often means that parents also have expectations of them when it comes to their children. Participants sometimes feel pressured to guide young people in difficult choices that the parents themselves struggle to address.
  • Not all women’s organizations are involved in support work. Yet they often end up doing so, as women’s organizations are seen as safe spaces where health and mental well-being can be discussed.
  • Because of the trust placed in them, these participants have become important figures for both young people and adults. However, this role is not always clear. Are they confidants, professionals, or activity facilitators? These roles often overlap.
  • Young people need space to express themselves, but you also want to be able to act preventively — for example, against violence or the possession of weapons.
  • Discussing taboos is essential, which means you also need to encourage open conversations about them. You do this not only preventively but also by setting an example.
  • An important lesson you can pass on is: “Stay true to who you are, even if you can’t change others.” You can inspire others simply by being yourself, especially when you are seen as a role model.

Step-By-Step Plan

  1. Step 1: Starting point

    The questions for help will clarify where you stand as an organization. What does the gap between young people and their parents look like within your organization? Is your organization a place where people can go if they are struggling with something or need help? The most important question is whether you want to be/become an organization that provides support. Discuss this with your teammates.

  2. Step 2: Set boundaries

    If people often come to you for a listening ear or confide in you, it is important to set boundaries. Sometimes certain issues can affect you deeply. And sometimes you end up spending much more time on them than you had anticipated. Set your own boundaries when it comes to your time and energy. It is okay to say no sometimes.

  3. Step 3: Create a safe environment

    Give people the space to tell their story. Above all, listen. Do not judge or condemn. Think together about how you prefer to interact and communicate with each other. You can draw up a number of rules together. For example: if you do not understand someone's story, ask questions and do not fill in the blanks for them.

  4. Step 4: Team building

    To bridge the gap between young people and their parents and strengthen the bond between them, you could organize more relaxed activities for both groups. These could include cooking together or a city trip in the Netherlands.

  5. Step 5: Coaches and sparring partners

    Are you often seen as a confidant, but do you sometimes find this role difficult? Then find a coach or sparring partner in your network who can help you with this.

  6. Step 6: Collaboration with professionals

    Sometimes it is useful to collaborate with the Municipal Health Service (GGD), a youth care nurse, or other care and support professionals. It is important to make clear agreements with each other. What kind of collaboration are you entering into? What exactly are you going to do? What exactly do you want to achieve? Who will perform which tasks? How much time will this take? And very importantly: what about financial compensation? Make sure you create as many agreements as possible.

  7. Step 7: Long term

    Bridging a gap and building trust is a process and takes time. Trial and error is part of that process. Don't be too hard on yourself if things don't go as you had hoped.
